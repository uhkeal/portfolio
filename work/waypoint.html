Concept case study / UX/UI design

Waypoint

Helping students and visitors find the right floor, room and route inside campus buildings, with step-free options built in.

Category
UX/UI Design · Mobile Product Design
Role
Solo Product Designer
Tools
Figma · Framer · Claude (AI assistant)
Scope
Concept project · one fictional building · 390×844 phone

An independent concept for indoor campus navigation, designed end to end in Figma. Hartwell Hall and Alder Campus are fictional, and the design has not been tested with users yet.

Explore the prototype ↗

02 / The problem

Outdoor maps end at the front door

Students and visitors can reach the right building and still struggle to find the right room. Inside, they have to read an unfamiliar layout, work out the right floor and choose a route that works for them.

01

Unfamiliar layouts

A first visit means working out corridors, wings and entrances with no map of the inside.

02

Finding rooms

Room names vary (“HH 214”, “Hartwell 214”, “214”) and say little about where the room actually is.

03

Accessibility

Step-free options can be hard to find, and directions often assume that stairs are fine.

The opportunity

Pick up where outdoor maps stop. Guide people from the entrance to the right floor and door, and offer a step-free route as an equal choice.

This framing is where the concept starts. It has not been researched with users yet.

03 / Users & journey

Designing around different ways of navigating

I wrote three personas to keep different needs in view, then mapped the path from opening the app to reaching the door.

The personas are design tools I wrote for this concept. They are not research participants.

Student

Maya, 19

Has four minutes to reach Room 214 in Hartwell Hall, a building she has never entered. She knows the building, but not the entrance, the layout or whether to take the stairs.

NeedsThe fastest route with the floor spelled out, and directions she can follow at a glance while walking.

Campus visitor

Daniel, 52

Arrives early for an information session in Hartwell Hall. He is not sure which entrance to use or how the floors are arranged.

NeedsPlain-language directions, and a clear sense of where he is relative to the destination.

Step-free route user

Sam, 24

Needs to reach Room 214. Before leaving the lobby, Sam wants to know which elevator to take and that the route avoids stairs.

NeedsA step-free route offered up front, with elevators and floor changes clearly shown.

From the front door to the right room

01

Search

User goalFind my room or building.

Likely frictionRoom names vary: “HH 214”, “Hartwell 214”, “214”.

Design opportunityForgiving search that accepts all three, with recent searches as shortcuts.

02

View floor plan

User goalUnderstand the building.

Likely frictionWhich floor? Where are the entrance and the elevator?

Design opportunityFloor selector, entrances and facilities appear before any routing starts.

03

Choose route

Highest risk

User goalPick a route that works for me.

Likely frictionStep-free options hide in settings; stairs versus elevator trade-offs are unclear.

Design opportunityStandard and step-free routes sit on one sheet, with time, distance and the reason for each.

04

Navigate

Highest risk

User goalKnow my next move.

Likely frictionFloor changes and wrong turns cause the most confusion; indoor positioning can drift.

Design opportunityOne instruction at a time, an explicit floor-change step and a Recenter button to correct the location (drawn in the wireframes only).

05

Arrive

User goalConfirm I am at the right door.

Likely frictionNo clear arrival moment; unsure which side the door is on.

Design opportunityAn arrival confirmation that names the room, floor and door side.

04 / Structure & wireframes

A simple structure, shaped before visual polish

Three primary tabs keep the experience shallow. I mapped the key flows, then used grayscale wireframes to settle hierarchy and screen structure before introducing the visual system.

05 / Visual direction & design system

A quiet map, one clear route

The visual direction prioritizes quick glances while walking: restrained surfaces, clear type hierarchy, a distinct route color and reusable components for maps, directions and controls.

Calm over clever

Soft neutrals, generous space and no decorative gradients. The floor plan is a quiet backdrop; the route is the only loud thing on screen.

Color carries meaning

Blue is the route, teal is step-free, and navy marks the destination and the instruction banner. Color is never decorative on the map.

Glanceable type

Instructions are 22 px, body text is 16 px, and 11 px is kept for map and tab labels only, so the most important text is the easiest to read.

Accessible by default

Text meets 4.5:1 contrast, control outlines reach 3:1 on white, touch targets are 44 px or larger, and step-free routing sits beside the standard route.

Design system

Colors, type and components live in Figma as variables, text styles and components, so all fifteen screens draw from one set.

Map styling

The floor plans use five quiet neutrals so nothing competes with the route. Hallways are white, rooms step through gray-blues, common space is a warm neutral, and blue and teal appear only on routes, connectors and the selected destination.

One documented refinement

Contrast pushed the palette darker

While checking contrast, two values fell short of the 4.5:1 target, so I changed them. The teal and status colors also ended up darker for the same reason.

Navigation Blue, with a white label: 4.0:1 to 4.8:1 #3978F6 → #2467F5
Secondary text on the page background: 4.4:1 to 5.1:1 #697586 → #5F6B7C

06 / Final experience

The key screens, and the decisions behind them

These key moments show how the interface supports orientation, route choice and step-free navigation.

01

Home

Why it is designed this way

Principle: Visual hierarchy

Search is the first thing on screen because the job is simple: get to this room. Recent places show their floor, so a repeat trip takes one tap, and the teal note introduces step-free routing before any route is chosen.

02

Search results

Why it is designed this way

Principle: Information scent

A room number should never compete with a building name, so the two are separated. Showing the floor up front answers the first indoor question before the person taps.

03

Building details and floor plan

Why it is designed this way

Principle: Recognition over recall

Floors, entrances and vertical circulation come before routing, so the person knows where the entrance and the elevator are. The floor switch changes the plan in place instead of adding a screen per floor.

04

Route preview and navigation

Why it is designed this way

Principle: Reduced cognitive load

The instruction sits in a high-contrast banner at the top, set at 22 px so it can be read at a glance while walking. Distance and time stay in the bottom panel. The “Demo · Next step” button simulates walking in the prototype; a real build would advance by indoor location.

05

Route selection and accessible route

Why it is designed this way

Principle: Error prevention

Step-free is a first-class choice made where the route is chosen. Teal always travels with an icon and the words “Step-free”, the 80 ft trade-off is stated in plain words, and navigation names Elevator B and the floor before you board.

06

Arrival

Why it is designed this way

Principle: Feedback

A route that simply ends leaves people checking door numbers. The last screen says which side the door is on and repeats the room and floor, so it is clear the trip is over.

Accessibility decisions

Step-free selection

A toggle on the route-selection sheet, “Step-free routes only”, sits where the route is chosen instead of in settings. Both routes list distance and time, and with the toggle on, a note says the step-free route is 80 ft longer.

Contrast

Text pairs were checked against the 4.5:1 minimum for text. A script over all 332 text runs on the fifteen screens found none below it, and the lowest is 4.7:1. Teal and blue are only 1.2:1 apart in lightness, so teal never carries meaning without an icon and words.

Readability

Body text is 16 px, supporting lines are 14 px and tags are 12 px. Instructions step up to 22 px so they can be read mid-stride, and 11 px appears only on map and tab labels. All 40 tappable elements are at least 44 × 44 px.

About these checks

These are my own design decisions and checks, run with scripts and contrast calculations. They are not an audit, a certification or a claim of WCAG conformance, and none has been tested with people. Screen-reader labels, large-text layouts, voice control and reduced motion are not designed in this concept.

Not user-tested · How I would validate it

How I would test it

This prototype hasn’t been tested with users. If I took it further, this is how I’d test it.

Task sessions

Students, campus visitors and people who use step-free routes each try three tasks while thinking aloud: find Hartwell 214, switch floors and choose a step-free route.

A short survey

Afterward, participants rate how easy each task was and how confident they felt following the route, and note anything that confused them.

Short interviews

A few participants would stay to talk about how they find rooms today, so the personas can be checked against real accounts.

What I would look for

Whether people finish each task, where they hesitate at the floor change, and whether they find the step-free option without being told.

Reflection

What worked

Treating step-free routing as a route choice shaped the whole flow. The toggle, the trade-off and the elevator name sit on the same screens everyone uses.

What I would change

The route preview shows the stairs route first, so someone who needs step-free has to open route options to switch. I would try asking earlier, or remembering the choice.

What a real build needs

Real floor-plan data, indoor positioning that holds up between floors, and designs for screen readers, large text and voice control. This concept covers none of them.
